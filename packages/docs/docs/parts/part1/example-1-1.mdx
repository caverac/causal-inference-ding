# Fisher's test and the p-value

Example 1.1 of the book ends with `fisher.test` reporting a p-value of $4.759 \times 10^{-5}$ for the resume
experiment. We explain what this number measures in that experiment by dealing the names again at random
(Figure 1) and by computing the exact distribution of the deals (Figure 2), and we close with what the number
does and does not say.

## The experiment

[Bertrand and Mullainathan (2004)](#ref-bertrand-2004) sent 4,870 fictitious resumes to help-wanted ads in
Boston and Chicago, each carrying a name chosen at random to sound White ($Z = 1$) or Black ($Z = 0$), and
recorded whether the employer called back ($Y = 1$). The 2,435 resumes with White-sounding names received 235
callbacks, against 157 for the 2,435 with Black-sounding names.

|         | $Y = 1$ | $Y = 0$ |
| :------ | ------: | ------: |
| $Z = 1$ |     235 |   2,200 |
| $Z = 0$ |     157 |   2,278 |

We write $X$ for the number of callbacks among the resumes with White-sounding names. Since each group has
2,435 resumes and the two groups share 392 callbacks, $X$ fixes the whole table,

|         |   $Y = 1$ |             $Y = 0$ |
| :------ | --------: | ------------------: |
| $Z = 1$ |       $X$ |         2,435 - $X$ |
| $Z = 0$ | 392 - $X$ | 2,435 - (392 - $X$) |

## The question the p-value answers

Some resumes are more attractive to employers than others, so the two groups would not receive the same number
of callbacks even if employers ignored the names, because chance decides which resumes carry which names. The
p-value asks whether "chance of this kind" can account for a gap as large as 235 against 157. To make the
phrase precise, we suppose that the name made no difference to any resume, so that each resume would have been
called back, or not, whatever name it carried. The 392 callbacks are then fixed, and the random assignment
only decides which pile each resume goes into, 2,435 with White-sounding names and 2,435 with Black-sounding
names, so that $X$ is the number of called-back resumes that land in the first pile when the names are dealt
at random.

The deals cluster around 196, half of the 392 callbacks, with a standard deviation of 9.5, and 99.3% of them

have the same size, so each called-back resume is equally likely to land in either.

## The exact probability

Counting deals only approximates the probabilities, which we compute exactly instead. Every deal is equally
likely, so the probability that $X = x$ is the fraction of deals that put $x$ of the 392 called-back resumes,
and $2435 - x$ of the 4,478 others, into the first pile,

$$
\mathrm{pr}(X = x) = \binom{392}{x} \binom{4478}{2435 - x} \bigg/ \binom{4870}{2435},
$$

which is the hypergeometric distribution of Figure 2.

<figcaption>

**Figure 2.** The computation of the p-value. The bars (top) are the exact probabilities $\mathrm{pr}(X = x)$
of each count if the names make no difference, and the points (bottom) are the same probabilities on a log
scale, where the tails become visible. The vertical line marks a count $x$, initially the observed 235, and
the p-value adds the probabilities of every count at least as unlikely as $x$, which lie on or below the
dashed line and are drawn filled. At 235 these are the two tails, $x \ge 235$ and $x \le 157$, which add up to
0.0000476. Moving the slider, or dragging across the chart, gives the p-value that other outcomes would have
had; at $x = 215$, for example, it is 0.0512.

</figcaption>

</Figure>

At the observed $x = 235$, the probability of a count at least as large is
$\mathrm{pr}(X \ge 235) = 0.0000238$. Counts as far off in the other direction are as unlikely, because the
two piles have the same size and the distribution is therefore symmetric about 196, so that
$\mathrm{pr}(X \le 157) = 0.0000238$ as well. The two-sided p-value adds both tails,

$$
p = \mathrm{pr}(X \ge 235) + \mathrm{pr}(X \le 157) = 0.0000476,
$$

which is the $4.759 \times 10^{-5}$ printed by `fisher.test`. In general, R adds the probabilities of all the
counts no more likely than the observed one, a rule that here selects the two tails. Dealing the names at
random and asking how often the result is as extreme is the Fisher randomization test of
[Chapter 3](../part2/chapter03.mdx), and Problem 3.4 shows that for a two-by-two table it coincides with
Fisher's exact test.

## Reading the number

The p-value states that, if the names made no difference to any resume, a random assignment of the names would
produce a gap at least as large as the observed one, in either direction, with probability 0.0000476, or about
5 times in 100,000. It is neither the probability that the names make no difference nor the probability that
the gap is due to chance, since the calculation assumes that the names make no difference; in the words of the
ASA statement, "P-values do not measure the probability that the studied hypothesis is true, or the
probability that the data were produced by random chance alone"
([Wasserstein and Lazar, 2016](#ref-wasserstein-2016)).

The p-value is not an effect size either, since the same p-value could come from a small effect measured on
many resumes. The size of the effect here is the difference in callback rates, 3.2 percentage points, and, as
the [ASA statement](#ref-wasserstein-2016) puts it, "A p-value, or statistical significance, does not measure
the size of an effect or the importance of a result." The calculation is exact because the names were assigned
at random, so that the deals are exactly the outcomes the experiment could have produced if the names made no
difference; without random assignment, the same arithmetic would lose this justification.

## The design, more precisely

The deals above treat the 4,870 resumes as one deck, as `fisher.test` does with the two-by-two table, whereas
the experiment was randomized more finely. Each ad typically received two higher-quality and two lower-quality
resumes, and one of each pair was drawn at random to receive an African-American name
([Bertrand and Mullainathan, 2004](#ref-bertrand-2004)). A test that follows this design deals the names
within each pair, as in the matched-pairs experiment of [Chapter 7](../part2/chapter07.mdx), and its p-value
could differ from the one above, although we have not computed it.
