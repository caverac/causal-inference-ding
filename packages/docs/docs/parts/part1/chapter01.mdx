## Problem 1.1: Independence in two-by-two tables

Write $p_z = \mathrm{pr}(Y = 1 \mid Z = z) = p_{z1}/(p_{z1} + p_{z0})$, so that $\mathrm{rd} = p_1 - p_0$ and
$\mathrm{rr} = p_1/p_0$. Both statements rest on two facts.

First, the three measures have the sign of $\Delta = p_{11} p_{00} - p_{10} p_{01}$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{rd} &= \frac{p_{11}}{p_{11} + p_{10}} - \frac{p_{01}}{p_{01} + p_{00}} \\
&= \frac{p_{11}(p_{01} + p_{00}) - p_{01}(p_{11} + p_{10})}{(p_{11} + p_{10})(p_{01} + p_{00})} \\
&= \frac{\Delta}{(p_{11} + p_{10})(p_{01} + p_{00})}, \\
\mathrm{rr} - 1 &= \frac{p_1 - p_0}{p_0} = \frac{\mathrm{rd}}{p_0}, \\
\mathrm{or} - 1 &= \frac{p_{11} p_{00} - p_{10} p_{01}}{p_{10} p_{01}} = \frac{\Delta}{p_{10} p_{01}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The denominators are positive whenever the measures are defined, so $\mathrm{rd}$, $\mathrm{rr} - 1$ and
$\mathrm{or} - 1$ all have the sign of $\Delta$.

Second, independence is equivalent to $p_1 = p_0$. If $Z \perp\!\!\!\perp Y$, then $p_z = \mathrm{pr}(Y = 1)$
for both $z$. Conversely, if $p_1 = p_0$, then

$$
\mathrm{pr}(Y = 1) = \mathrm{pr}(Z = 1)\, p_1 + \mathrm{pr}(Z = 0)\, p_0 = p_1,
$$

so $\mathrm{pr}(Y = y \mid Z = z) = \mathrm{pr}(Y = y)$ for every $z$ and $y$.

### a. Statement (1)

Assume that rd, rr and or are defined, that is, no denominator vanishes. By the two facts,

$$
Z \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{rd} = 0 \Leftrightarrow \Delta = 0 \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{rr} = 1 \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{or} = 1. \qquad \blacksquare
$$

### b. Statement (2)

Every $p_{zy} > 0$, so the measures are defined. By the first fact,

$$
\mathrm{rd} > 0 \Leftrightarrow \Delta > 0 \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{rr} > 1 \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{or} > 1. \qquad \blacksquare
$$

For the table of [Example 1.1](./example-1-1.mdx), $235 \times 2278 - 2200 \times 157 = 189{,}930 > 0$, and
indeed $\mathrm{rd} = 0.032$, $\mathrm{rr} = 1.50$ and $\mathrm{or} = 1.55$.

## Problem 1.2: More examples of the Yule-Simpson Paradox

### a. A numeric example

I use the geometry of Section 1.3.3. In the subgroup $X = x$, the treated arm is the vector
$A_x = (n_{10|x}, n_{11|x})$ of failures and successes, and the control arm is $B_x = (n_{00|x}, n_{01|x})$.
The aggregated arms are $A = A_1 + A_0$ and $B = B_1 + B_0$. The slope of a vector is the odds of success,
which increases with the success rate. Thus $\mathrm{rd}_x > 0$ exactly when $A_x$ is steeper than $B_x$, and
$\mathrm{rd} > 0$ exactly when $A$ is steeper than $B$.

By the law of total probability,

$$
\mathrm{pr}(Y = 1 \mid Z = z) = \sum_x \mathrm{pr}(X = x \mid Z = z)\, \mathrm{pr}(Y = 1 \mid Z = z, X = x),
$$

so the direction of $A$ lies between those of $A_1$ and $A_0$, closer to the subgroup with more treated units,
and likewise for $B$. A reversal needs the two arms to divide differently between the subgroups. If
$\mathrm{pr}(X = x \mid Z = 1) = \mathrm{pr}(X = x \mid Z = 0) = w_x$, then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{rd} &= \sum_x w_x \{\mathrm{pr}(Y = 1 \mid Z = 1, X = x) - \mathrm{pr}(Y = 1 \mid Z = 0, X = x)\} \\
&= \sum_x w_x\, \mathrm{rd}_x,
\end{aligned}
$$

which has the sign that the $\mathrm{rd}_x$ share.

I take a postcard ($Z = 1$) mailed to 110 customers and withheld from 110 others ($Z = 0$), with $Y = 1$ if
the customer buys and $X$ the segment of a purchase propensity model, $X = 1$ for high and $X = 0$ for low. I
put 100 of the mailed customers in the high segment and 100 of the held-out customers in the low one. In the
high segment 50 of the 100 mailed customers buy and 6 of the 10 held out; in the low segment 1 of the 10
mailed customers buys and 20 of the 100 held out. Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{rd}_1 &= \frac{50}{100} - \frac{6}{10} = -0.100, \\
\mathrm{rd}_0 &= \frac{1}{10} - \frac{20}{100} = -0.100, \\
\mathrm{rd} &= \frac{51}{110} - \frac{26}{110} = 0.227.
\end{aligned}
$$

The postcard lowers buying in each segment and raises it overall.

```r
> unlist(postcard$risk_difference)

-0.1000000 -0.1000000  0.2272727
> postcard$sign_change_at
[1] 0.3409091
```

### b. A real-life example

[Crook et al. (2009)](#ref-crook-2009) report that occurrences of Simpson's paradox in online A/B tests "are
not uncommon, and we have seen them happen multiple times in real life". Such tests often start with a small
share of visitors in the treatment and raise it later. Their simple example has one million visitors on each
of two days, with the treatment ($Z = 1$) receiving 1% of the visitors on Friday ($X = 1$) and 50% on Saturday
($X = 0$), and $Y = 1$ a conversion:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{rd}_1 &= \frac{230}{10000} - \frac{20000}{990000} = 0.0028, \\
\mathrm{rd}_0 &= \frac{6000}{500000} - \frac{5000}{500000} = 0.0020, \\
\mathrm{rd} &= \frac{6230}{510000} - \frac{25000}{1490000} = -0.0046.
\end{aligned}
$$

The treatment converts better on each day and worse overall.

:::warning[Discrepancy in Crook et al. (2009)]

Their Table 1 prints 1.20% for the overall conversion rate of the treatment, whereas $6230/510000 = 1.22\%$.
The reversal is unaffected.

:::

$A_1$ is a stub at the origin, since the treatment received only 10,000 of its 510,000 visitors on Friday, so

so $B$ leans toward $B_1$. Saturday has the lower conversion rates, and it carries 98% of the visitors of the
treatment but 34% of those of the control. The reversal holds while $\mathrm{pr}(X = 1 \mid Z = 1) < 0.434$,
so a treatment share held fixed across both days removes it. The numbers are the simple example of the paper,
which illustrates a pattern the authors report from practice.

## Problem 1.3: Correlation and partial correlation

### a. The partial correlation

Assume $|\rho_{XY}| < 1$ and $|\rho_{XZ}| < 1$. Appendix A.1.2 with $Y_1 = (Y, Z)$ and $Y_2 = X$ gives
$\Sigma_{22} = 1$ and $\Sigma_{12} = (\rho_{XY}, \rho_{XZ})^\top$, so the covariance matrix of $(Y, Z)$ given
$X = x$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12} \Sigma_{22}^{-1} \Sigma_{21}
&= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho_{YZ} \\ \rho_{YZ} & 1 \end{pmatrix}
- \begin{pmatrix} \rho_{XY} \\ \rho_{XZ} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \rho_{XY} & \rho_{XZ} \end{pmatrix} \\
&= \begin{pmatrix} 1 - \rho_{XY}^2 & \rho_{YZ} - \rho_{XY} \rho_{XZ} \\ \rho_{YZ} - \rho_{XY} \rho_{XZ} & 1 - \rho_{XZ}^2 \end{pmatrix}.
\end{aligned}
$$

It does not depend on $x$; only the conditional mean $(\rho_{XY} x, \rho_{XZ} x)$ does. Dividing the
off-diagonal entry by the square roots of the diagonal ones, with $\rho_{YX} = \rho_{XY}$ and
$\rho_{ZX} = \rho_{XZ}$, we have

$$
\rho_{YZ|X} = \frac{\rho_{YZ} - \rho_{YX} \rho_{ZX}}{\sqrt{1 - \rho_{YX}^2} \sqrt{1 - \rho_{ZX}^2}}. \qquad \blacksquare
$$

The assumption keeps the conditional variances positive. If $X$ is uncorrelated with both variables,
$\rho_{YX} = \rho_{ZX} = 0$ and $\rho_{YZ|X} = \rho_{YZ}$, as expected. The same formula also follows from
correlating the residuals of the least squares regressions of $Y$ and $Z$ on $X$.

### b. A numerical example

The denominator is positive, so $\rho_{YZ} > 0 > \rho_{YZ|X}$ exactly when
$0 < \rho_{YZ} < \rho_{YX} \rho_{ZX}$. I take $\rho_{XY} = \rho_{XZ} = 0.6$ and $\rho_{YZ} = 0.2 < 0.36$:

$$
\rho_{YZ|X} = \frac{0.2 - 0.36}{1 - 0.36} = -0.25.
$$

The covariance matrix is positive definite, since its leading principal minors are $1$, $1 - 0.36 = 0.64$ and
$1 + 2 (0.6)(0.6)(0.2) - 0.36 - 0.36 - 0.04 = 0.384$. The covariance of $Y$ and $Z$ splits into $0.36$ carried
by $X$ and the conditional covariance $-0.16$, which is why the sign flips once $X$ is fixed. I check the
value by simulation, with the columns of `draws` ordered as $(X, Y, Z)$:

```r
> pkgload::load_all("packages/causalding")
> partial_correlation(0.2, 0.6, 0.6)
[1] -0.25
> sigma <- matrix(c(1, 0.6, 0.6, 0.6, 1, 0.2, 0.6, 0.2, 1), 3)
> set.seed(1)
> draws <- matrix(rnorm(3e6), ncol = 3) %*% chol(sigma)
> cor(resid(lm(draws[, 2] ~ draws[, 1])), resid(lm(draws[, 3] ~ draws[, 1])))
[1] -0.2503809
```

## Problem 1.4: Specification searches

### a. The 1024 regressions

Of the 1024 coefficients of `treat`, 260 are positively significant, 80 are negatively significant and 684 are
not significant. Each regression keeps `treat` and adds a subset $\{X_1, \dots, X_k\}$ of `age`, `educ`,
`black`, `hispan`, `married`, `nodegree`, `re74`, `re75`, `u74` and `u75`,

$$
\texttt{re78} = \alpha + \beta \, \texttt{treat} + \gamma_1 X_1 + \dots + \gamma_k X_k + \varepsilon,
$$

fitted by least squares. Each covariate is in or out, so there are $2^{10} = 1024$ regressions, from
`lm(re78 ~ treat)` to `lm(re78 ~ .)`. I call $\beta$ significant when the two-sided p-value of
`summary(lm(...))` is below 0.05, as in Section 1.2.1.

The two regressions of Section 1.2.1 sit far apart, at $-8{,}506$ without covariates, negatively significant,
and at $1{,}068$ with all ten, with $p = 0.054$. The p-values use the homoskedastic standard errors of

0.05, such as the one with all ten covariates, across the threshold. The search covers additive linear models
only, so a wider search would spread the estimates at least as much.

### b. Other findings

The earnings history decides the sign. All 64 regressions without `re74`, `re75`, `u74` and `u75` are
negatively significant, from $-8{,}526$ to $-2{,}882$, while 809 of the 960 with at least one of them are
positive, from $-2{,}682$ to $2{,}954$. With `re75` in the regression, none of the 512 coefficients is
negatively significant, against 80 of the 512 without it.

The omitted variable formula explains why. Write $Y$ for `re78`, $Z$ for `treat` and $X$ for one covariate.
Adding $X$ alone to the regression of $Y$ on $Z$ changes the coefficient of $Z$ by
$\Delta\beta = -\gamma\delta$, where $\gamma$ is the coefficient of $X$ in the regression of $Y$ on $Z$ and
$X$, and $\delta$ that of $Z$ in the regression of $X$ on $Z$. In sample correlations,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\delta &= \rho_{ZX} \frac{\mathrm{sd}(X)}{\mathrm{sd}(Z)}, \\
\gamma &= \rho_{YX|Z} \frac{\mathrm{sd}(Y) \sqrt{1 - \rho_{YZ}^2}}{\mathrm{sd}(X) \sqrt{1 - \rho_{ZX}^2}}, \\
\Delta\beta &= -K \frac{\rho_{ZX} \rho_{YX|Z}}{\sqrt{1 - \rho_{ZX}^2}}, \qquad K = \frac{\mathrm{sd}(Y)}{\mathrm{sd}(Z)} \sqrt{1 - \rho_{YZ}^2},
\end{aligned}
$$

with $\rho_{YX|Z}$ the partial correlation of Problem 1.3. $K$ is the same for every covariate, so the plane
of $\rho_{ZX}$ and $\rho_{YX|Z}$ maps $\Delta\beta$ (Figure 4b), and $\Delta\beta > 0$ when the two
correlations have opposite signs. The treated units earned less before the program, with $\rho_{ZX} = -0.13$
for `re74` and $-0.14$ for `re75`, and earlier earnings predict `re78`, with $\rho_{YX|Z} = 0.64$ and $0.67$.
Thus `re75` alone moves the coefficient from $-8{,}506$ to $-72$.

## Problem 1.5: More on racial discrimination

The racial gap is the same for female and male names, about 3 percentage points, but only the female gap is
significant at 5%, because male names are less than a quarter of the resumes. I split the resumes of
[Example 1.1](./example-1-1.mdx) by the sex of the name and repeat its analysis in each part, with $Z = 1$ for
a White-sounding name and $Y = 1$ for a callback. The risk differences are in percentage points, with standard
errors in parentheses, and the p-values are those of `fisher.test`.

| Names  |           $Z = 1$ |           $Z = 0$ | $\widehat{\mathrm{rd}}$ |  p-value |
| :----- | ----------------: | ----------------: | ----------------------: | -------: |
| female | 184/1,860 = 9.89% | 125/1,886 = 6.63% |             3.26 (0.90) |  0.00029 |
| male   |    51/575 = 8.87% |    32/549 = 5.83% |             3.04 (1.55) |    0.053 |
| all    | 235/2,435 = 9.65% | 157/2,435 = 6.45% |             3.20 (0.78) | 0.000048 |

```r
> pkgload::load_all("packages/causalding")
> by_sex <- resume_by_sex()
> print(by_sex$groups[c("sex", "risk_difference", "std_error", "p_value")], digits = 2)
     sex risk_difference std_error p_value
1 female           0.033    0.0090 2.9e-04
2   male           0.030    0.0155 5.3e-02
3    all           0.032    0.0078 4.8e-05
> print(unlist(by_sex$difference), digits = 2)
 estimate std_error   p_value
   0.0022    0.0179    0.9006
```

White-sounding names are called back about 1.5 times as often as Black-sounding ones, among female and male
names alike. A standard error scales as $1/\sqrt{n}$, and the 3,746 resumes with female names outnumber the
1,124 with male names by a factor of $\sim 3.3$, which accounts for most of the ratio $1.55/0.90 \approx 1.7$
of the standard errors. The two gaps differ by 0.22 points with a standard error of 1.79 and $p = 0.90$, so
the data show no difference between the sexes. The difference between a significant and an insignificant
result is not itself significant ([Gelman and Stern, 2006](#ref-gelman-2006)). The last row reproduces the
p-value of Example 1.1.

Race was randomized within each sex, so each row is a randomized comparison. Sex was not randomized, since
male names went almost only to sales jobs and female names to both sales and administrative jobs
([Bertrand and Mullainathan, 2003](#ref-bertrand-2003)), so comparing the sexes compares different jobs. The
p-values also treat the resumes as independent, whereas the names were randomized within pairs of resumes sent
to the same ad. A test that follows the pairs could move the male p-value, at 0.053, to either side of 0.05; I
have not computed it.
