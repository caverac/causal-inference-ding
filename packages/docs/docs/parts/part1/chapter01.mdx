# Chapter 1: Correlation, Association, and the Yule-Simpson Paradox

## Problem 1.1: Independence in two-by-two tables

Let $p_z = \mathrm{pr}(Y = 1 \mid Z = z) = p_{z1}/(p_{z1} + p_{z0})$, so $\mathrm{rd} = p_1 - p_0$ and
$\mathrm{rr} = p_1/p_0$. Part (1) assumes rd, rr and or are defined (no zero denominators); in part (2) this
holds since every $p_{zy} > 0$.

**Step 1: the measures share a sign.** Let $\Delta = p_{11} p_{00} - p_{10} p_{01}$. Then

$$
\mathrm{rd} = \frac{\Delta}{(p_{11} + p_{10})(p_{01} + p_{00})}, \qquad \mathrm{rr} - 1 = \frac{\mathrm{rd}}{p_0}, \qquad \mathrm{or} - 1 = \frac{\Delta}{p_{10} p_{01}}.
$$

The denominators are positive, so $\mathrm{rd}$, $\mathrm{rr} - 1$ and $\mathrm{or} - 1$ all have the sign of
$\Delta$.

**Step 2: independence means $p_1 = p_0$.** If $Z \perp\!\!\!\perp Y$, then $p_z = \mathrm{pr}(Y = 1)$ for
both $z$. Conversely, if $p_1 = p_0$, then

$$
\mathrm{pr}(Y = 1) = \mathrm{pr}(Z = 1)\, p_1 + \mathrm{pr}(Z = 0)\, p_0 = p_1,
$$

so $\mathrm{pr}(Y = y \mid Z = z) = \mathrm{pr}(Y = y)$ for every $z$ and $y$, which is independence.

### Part (1)

By Steps 2 and 1,

$$
Z \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{rd} = 0 \Leftrightarrow \Delta = 0 \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{rr} = 1 \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{or} = 1. \qquad \blacksquare
$$

### Part (2)

Every $p_{zy} > 0$, so the measures are defined, and by Step 1,

$$
\mathrm{rd} > 0 \Leftrightarrow \Delta > 0 \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{rr} > 1 \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{or} > 1. \qquad \blacksquare
$$

## Problem 1.2: More examples of the Yule-Simpson Paradox

Although not necessary, I tried to use the same geometric interpreation of Section 1.3.3 for this problem, I
found it useful! In the subgroup $X = x$, draw the treated arm as the vector $A_x = (n_{10|x}, n_{11|x})$, and
the control arm as $B_x = (n_{00|x}, n_{01|x})$ (in both cases, we are plotting (failures, successes)). The
aggregated arms are the sums $A = A_1 + A_0$ and $B = B_1 + B_0$, the diagonals of two parallelograms. Let
$\mathrm{rd}_x$ be the risk difference within $X = x$.

The slope of $A_x$ is the odds $n_{11|x}/n_{10|x}$: a steepter slope has a higher success rate. So
$\mathrm{rd}_x > 0$ exactly when $A_x$ is steeper than $B_x$, and $\mathrm{rd} > 0$ exactly when $A$ is
steeper than $B$.

Since

$$
\mathrm{pr}(Y = 1 \mid Z = 1) = \sum_x \mathrm{pr}(X = x \mid Z = 1)\, \mathrm{pr}(Y = 1 \mid Z = 1, X = x),
$$

the direction of $A$ lies between those of $A_1$ and $A_0$, and moving treated units from $X = 0$ to $X = 1$
turns $A$ toward $A_1$. The same holds for $B$.

The paradox is then a matter of lengths. Take one subgroup whose vectors are steep and one whose vectors are
flat, with $B_x$ steeper than $A_x$ in both. Put most treated units in the steep subgroup and most control
units in the flat one: $A$ then points close to the steep direction, $B$ close to the flat one, and $A$ is
steeper than $B$.

It takes different mixes. If $\mathrm{pr}(X = 1 \mid Z = 1) = \mathrm{pr}(X = 1 \mid Z = 0)$, then

$$
\mathrm{rd} = \mathrm{pr}(X = 1)\, \mathrm{rd}_1 + \mathrm{pr}(X = 0)\, \mathrm{rd}_0,
$$

which has the sign that $\mathrm{rd}_1$ and $\mathrm{rd}_0$ share.

### A numeric example

A postcard ($Z = 1$) is mailed to 110 customers and withheld from 110 others ($Z = 0$); $Y = 1$ if the
customer buys, and $X$ is the customer's segment from a purchase propensity model, $X = 1$ for high and
$X = 0$ for low. The counts are the tables of Figure 1: in the high segment, 50 of the 100 mailed customers
buy and 6 of the 10 held out; in the low segment, 1 of the 10 mailed and 20 of the 100 held out.

Within each segment $B_x$ is the steeper vector, $\mathrm{rd}_1 = \mathrm{rd}_0 = -0.100$: the postcard lowers
buying. But 100 of the 110 mailed customers are in the high segment and 100 of the 110 held out are in the low
one, so $A$ is nearly $A_1$, $B$ is nearly $B_0$, and

$$
\mathrm{rd} = \frac{51}{110} - \frac{26}{110} = 0.227.
$$

The reversal holds while $\mathrm{pr}(X = 1 \mid Z = 1) > 0.341$, against $10/110 = 0.091$ for the customers
held out. With the same mix, $\mathrm{rd} = -0.100$.

### A real-life example

Online A/B tests often start with a small share of visitors in the treatment and raise it later.
[Crook et al. (2009)](#ref-crook-2009) report that occurrences of Simpson's paradox in such tests "are not
uncommon, and we have seen them happen multiple times in real life", and illustrate it with conversion rates
over two days, the treatment ($Z = 1$) receiving 1% of visitors on Friday ($X = 1$) and 50% on Saturday
($X = 0$), with $Y = 1$ a conversion. The counts are the tables of Figure 2: on Friday, 230 conversions among
the 10,000 visitors in the treatment and 20,000 among the 990,000 in the control; on Saturday, 6,000 and 5,000
among 500,000 each.

<Figure id="figure-2">

<SimpsonGeometryExplorer name="ramp_up" />

<figcaption>

**Figure 2.** The ramp-up, laid out as [Figure 1](#figure-1): the counts on top, and below them the plane,
visitors without a conversion across, conversions up. Conversion rates are near 1%, so every vector is nearly
flat and the vertical axis is stretched; scaling an axis keeps sums of vectors and the order of slopes. The
controls work as in Figure 1.

</figcaption>

</Figure>

On each day $A_x$ is the steeper vector, $\mathrm{rd}_1 = 0.0028$ and $\mathrm{rd}_0 = 0.0020$: the treatment
converts better. But it received 10,000 of its 510,000 visitors on Friday, so $A_1$ is a stub at the origin
and $A$ is nearly $A_0$, the flatter Saturday direction, while the control received 990,000 of its 1,490,000
on Friday and $B$ leans toward the steeper $B_1$. So

$$
\mathrm{rd} = \frac{6230}{510000} - \frac{25000}{1490000} = -0.0046.
$$

The treatment converts better on each day and worse overall. The reversal holds while
$\mathrm{pr}(X = 1 \mid Z = 1) < 0.434$; a treatment share of traffic that stays the same on both days would
give both arms the same mix. (The paper prints 1.20% for 6,230/510,000, which is 1.22%.)

## Problem 1.3: Correlation and partial correlation

Assume $|\rho_{XY}| < 1$ and $|\rho_{XZ}| < 1$, so that $Y$ and $Z$ keep a positive variance given $X$.

**Step 1: the conditional covariance.** Apply Appendix A.1.2 with $Y_1 = (Y, Z)$ and $Y_2 = X$, so that
$\Sigma_{22} = 1$ and $\Sigma_{12} = (\rho_{XY}, \rho_{XZ})^\top$. Given $X = x$, the covariance matrix of
$(Y, Z)$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12} \Sigma_{22}^{-1} \Sigma_{21}
&= \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \rho_{YZ} \\ \rho_{YZ} & 1 \end{pmatrix}
- \begin{pmatrix} \rho_{XY} \\ \rho_{XZ} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \rho_{XY} & \rho_{XZ} \end{pmatrix} \\
&= \begin{pmatrix} 1 - \rho_{XY}^2 & \rho_{YZ} - \rho_{XY} \rho_{XZ} \\ \rho_{YZ} - \rho_{XY} \rho_{XZ} & 1 - \rho_{XZ}^2 \end{pmatrix},
\end{aligned}
$$

which does not involve $x$; only the conditional mean, $(\rho_{XY} x, \rho_{XZ} x)$, does.

**Step 2: the correlation.** Divide the covariance by the product of the standard deviations, and write
$\rho_{YX} = \rho_{XY}$, $\rho_{ZX} = \rho_{XZ}$:

$$
\rho_{YZ|X} = \frac{\rho_{YZ} - \rho_{YX} \rho_{ZX}}{\sqrt{1 - \rho_{YX}^2} \sqrt{1 - \rho_{ZX}^2}}. \qquad \blacksquare
$$

**Numerical example.** The denominator is positive, so $\rho_{YZ} > 0 > \rho_{YZ|X}$ exactly when
$0 < \rho_{YZ} < \rho_{YX} \rho_{ZX}$. Take $\rho_{XY} = \rho_{XZ} = 0.6$ and $\rho_{YZ} = 0.2 < 0.36$:

$$
\rho_{YZ|X} = \frac{0.2 - 0.36}{1 - 0.36} = -0.25.
$$

The covariance matrix is positive definite, with leading principal minors $1$, $1 - 0.36 = 0.64$ and
$1 + 2 (0.6)(0.6)(0.2) - 0.36 - 0.36 - 0.04 = 0.384$, so this Normal vector exists. $Y$ and $Z$ are positively
correlated, yet negatively correlated given $X = x$, whatever the value of $x$.

## Problem 1.4: Specification searches

The ten covariates are `age`, `educ`, `black`, `hispan`, `married`, `nodegree`, `re74`, `re75`, `u74` and
`u75`. Each regression keeps `treat` and adds a subset $\{X_1, \dots, X_k\}$ of them,

$$
\texttt{re78} = \alpha + \beta \, \texttt{treat} + \gamma_1 X_1 + \dots + \gamma_k X_k + \varepsilon,
$$

fitted by least squares. Each covariate is either in or out, so there are $2^{10} = 1024$ regressions, from
none of the covariates, `lm(re78 ~ treat)`, to all ten, `lm(re78 ~ .)`: the two regressions of Section 1.2.1.
The coefficient $\beta$ of `treat` is significant when the two-sided p-value that `summary(lm(...))` reports
for it is below 0.05. The function `specification_search()` of the `causalding` package runs them.

| Coefficient of `treat` | Regressions |
| :--------------------- | ----------: |
| positively significant |         260 |
| negatively significant |          80 |
| not significant        |         684 |

<SpecificationCurve />

<figcaption>

**Figure 3.** The 1024 regressions as a specification curve ([Simonsohn et al., 2020](#ref-simonsohn-2020)).
Top: the coefficient of `treat` in each regression with its 95% confidence interval, by increasing value;
rings mark the two regressions of Section 1.2.1. Bottom: the regressions whose coefficient is significant at
5%, then the covariates each regression includes, one row per covariate. A toggle brings forward the
regressions that include its covariate, and the readout counts them with and without it; pointing at a
regression reads its coefficient.

</figcaption>

Things to notice:

**The earnings history decides the sign.** All 64 regressions without `re74`, `re75`, `u74` and `u75` give a
negatively significant coefficient, from $-8{,}526$ to $-2{,}882$. Of the 960 with at least one of them, 809
give a positive coefficient, and their estimates run from $-2{,}682$ to $2{,}954$.

**Why the earnings history matters.** Write $Y$ for `re78`, $Z$ for `treat` and $X$ for one covariate. Adding
$X$ alone to the regression of $Y$ on $Z$ changes the coefficient of $Z$ by

$$
\Delta\beta = -K \frac{\rho_{ZX} \rho_{YX|Z}}{\sqrt{1 - \rho_{ZX}^2}}, \qquad
K = \frac{\mathrm{sd}(Y)}{\mathrm{sd}(Z)} \sqrt{1 - \rho_{YZ}^2},
$$

the omitted variable formula written with sample correlations, $\rho_{YX|Z}$ being the partial correlation of
Problem 1.3. $K$ is the same for every covariate, so the plane of $\rho_{ZX}$ and $\rho_{YX|Z}$ is a map of
$\Delta\beta$ (Figure 4b): a covariate raises the coefficient exactly when the two correlations have opposite
signs. The treated units earned less before the program and their earlier earnings predict `re78`, so the
earnings history lies farthest out on this map; `re75` alone moves the coefficient from $-8{,}506$ to $-72$.

<figcaption>

**Figure 4.** The correlations behind Figure 3. **a**, the sample correlations of the twelve variables,
grouped as outcome and treatment, earnings history and demographics: the area of a circle is the absolute

**The regressions of Section 1.2.1 disagree.** Without covariates the coefficient is $-8{,}506$, negatively
significant; with all ten it is $1{,}068$ with $p = 0.054$, not significant at 5%.

**The search can support any conclusion.** Choosing among the 1024 after seeing them can report a significant
loss, a significant gain or no effect. Section 10.4.2 of the book calls reporting a favorable estimate "after
searching over a wide set of candidate models, without confessing this model searching process" "a major
source of p-hacking in causal inference", following [Leamer (1978)](#ref-leamer-1978). For comparison, the
experiment of Section 4.5.3, with the same 185 treated units and randomized controls, estimates $1{,}794$.

## Problem 1.5: More on racial discrimination

Split the resumes of [Example 1.1](./example-1-1.mdx) by the sex of the name and repeat its analysis in each
part, with $Z = 1$ for a White-sounding name and $Y = 1$ for a callback. The risk differences are in
percentage points, with their standard errors in parentheses, and the p-values are those of `fisher.test`.

| Names  |           $Z = 1$ |           $Z = 0$ | $\widehat{\mathrm{rd}}$ |  p-value |
| :----- | ----------------: | ----------------: | ----------------------: | -------: |
| female | 184/1,860 = 9.89% | 125/1,886 = 6.63% |             3.26 (0.90) |  0.00029 |
| male   |    51/575 = 8.87% |    32/549 = 5.83% |             3.04 (1.55) |    0.053 |
| all    | 235/2,435 = 9.65% | 157/2,435 = 6.45% |             3.20 (0.78) | 0.000048 |

1. The gap is the same for both sexes. White-sounding names are called back about 3 percentage points more
   often, or about 1.5 times as often, among female and male names alike.

2. Only the gap for female names is significant. Male names are less than a quarter of the resumes, so the
   same gap has a standard error almost twice as large and $p = 0.053$. This is a loss of precision, not a
   smaller effect: the two gaps differ by 0.22 points with a standard error of 1.79, $p = 0.90$. "Significant
   for female names, not for male names" does not show that the names matter more for women, since the
   difference between a significant and an insignificant result is not itself significant
   ([Gelman and Stern, 2006](#ref-gelman-2006)).

3. Within each sex the comparison is an experiment; between the sexes it is not. The race of the name was
   assigned at random within each sex, so each row of the table is a randomized comparison. The sex was not:
   male names were used almost only for sales jobs, and female names for both sales and administrative jobs
   ([Bertrand and Mullainathan, 2003](#ref-bertrand-2003)), so the callback rates of male and female names
   compare different jobs.
