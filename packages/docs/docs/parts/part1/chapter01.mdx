# Chapter 1: Correlation, Association, and the Yule-Simpson Paradox

{/* PROBLEM 1.1 */}

## Problem 1.1: Independence in two-by-two tables

Let $p_z = \mathrm{pr}(Y = 1 \mid Z = z) = p_{z1}/(p_{z1} + p_{z0})$, so $\mathrm{rd} = p_1 - p_0$ and
$\mathrm{rr} = p_1/p_0$. Part (1) assumes rd, rr and or are defined (no zero denominators); in part (2) this
holds since every $p_{zy} > 0$.

**Step 1: the measures share a sign.** Let $\Delta = p_{11} p_{00} - p_{10} p_{01}$. Then

$$
\mathrm{rd} = \frac{\Delta}{(p_{11} + p_{10})(p_{01} + p_{00})}, \qquad \mathrm{rr} - 1 = \frac{\mathrm{rd}}{p_0}, \qquad \mathrm{or} - 1 = \frac{\Delta}{p_{10} p_{01}}.
$$

The denominators are positive, so $\mathrm{rd}$, $\mathrm{rr} - 1$ and $\mathrm{or} - 1$ all have the sign of
$\Delta$.

**Step 2: independence means $p_1 = p_0$.** If $Z \perp\!\!\!\perp Y$, then $p_z = \mathrm{pr}(Y = 1)$ for
both $z$. Conversely, if $p_1 = p_0$, then

$$
\mathrm{pr}(Y = 1) = \mathrm{pr}(Z = 1)\, p_1 + \mathrm{pr}(Z = 0)\, p_0 = p_1,
$$

so $\mathrm{pr}(Y = y \mid Z = z) = \mathrm{pr}(Y = y)$ for every $z$ and $y$, which is independence.

### Part (1)

By Steps 2 and 1,

$$
Z \perp\!\!\!\perp Y \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{rd} = 0 \Leftrightarrow \Delta = 0 \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{rr} = 1 \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{or} = 1. \qquad \blacksquare
$$

### Part (2)

Every $p_{zy} > 0$, so the measures are defined, and by Step 1,

$$
\mathrm{rd} > 0 \Leftrightarrow \Delta > 0 \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{rr} > 1 \Leftrightarrow \mathrm{or} > 1. \qquad \blacksquare
$$

## Problem 1.2: More examples of the Yule-Simpson Paradox

### The geometry of Section 1.3.3

In the subgroup $X = x$, draw the treated arm as the vector $A_x = (n_{10|x}, n_{11|x})$ of its failures and
successes, and the control arm as $B_x = (n_{00|x}, n_{01|x})$. The aggregated arms are the sums
$A = A_1 + A_0$ and $B = B_1 + B_0$, the diagonals of two parallelograms. Let $\mathrm{rd}_x$ be the risk
difference within $X = x$.

**A steeper vector has a higher success rate.** The slope of $A_x$ is the odds $n_{11|x}/n_{10|x}$, which
increases with $\mathrm{pr}(Y = 1 \mid Z = 1, X = x)$. So $\mathrm{rd}_x > 0$ exactly when $A_x$ is steeper
than $B_x$, and $\mathrm{rd} > 0$ exactly when $A$ is steeper than $B$.

**A sum points between its terms, toward the one with more units.** By the law of total probability,

$$
\mathrm{pr}(Y = 1 \mid Z = 1) = \sum_x \mathrm{pr}(X = x \mid Z = 1)\, \mathrm{pr}(Y = 1 \mid Z = 1, X = x),
$$

so the direction of $A$ lies between those of $A_1$ and $A_0$, and moving treated units from $X = 0$ to
$X = 1$ turns $A$ toward $A_1$. The same holds for $B$.

The paradox is then a matter of lengths. Take one subgroup whose vectors are steep and one whose vectors are
flat, with $B_x$ steeper than $A_x$ in both. Put most treated units in the steep subgroup and most control
units in the flat one: $A$ then points close to the steep direction, $B$ close to the flat one, and $A$ is
steeper than $B$.

It takes different mixes. If $\mathrm{pr}(X = 1 \mid Z = 1) = \mathrm{pr}(X = 1 \mid Z = 0)$, then

$$
\mathrm{rd} = \mathrm{pr}(X = 1)\, \mathrm{rd}_1 + \mathrm{pr}(X = 0)\, \mathrm{rd}_0,
$$

which has the sign that $\mathrm{rd}_1$ and $\mathrm{rd}_0$ share.

### A numeric example

A postcard ($Z = 1$) is mailed to 110 customers and withheld from 110 others ($Z = 0$); $Y = 1$ if the
customer buys, and $X$ is the customer's segment from a purchase propensity model, $X = 1$ for high and
$X = 0$ for low.

| $X$      | $Z$ | Customers | Buyers | $\mathrm{pr}(Y = 1 \mid Z, X)$ |
| :------- | --: | --------: | -----: | -----------------------------: |
| 1 (high) |   1 |       100 |     50 |                          0.500 |
| 1 (high) |   0 |        10 |      6 |                          0.600 |
| 0 (low)  |   1 |        10 |      1 |                          0.100 |
| 0 (low)  |   0 |       100 |     20 |                          0.200 |

Within each segment $B_x$ is the steeper vector, $\mathrm{rd}_1 = \mathrm{rd}_0 = -0.100$: the postcard lowers
buying. But 100 of the 110 mailed customers are in the high segment and 100 of the 110 held out are in the low
one, so $A$ is nearly $A_1$, $B$ is nearly $B_0$, and

$$
\mathrm{rd} = \frac{51}{110} - \frac{26}{110} = 0.227.
$$

The reversal holds while $\mathrm{pr}(X = 1 \mid Z = 1) > 0.341$, against $10/110 = 0.091$ for the customers
held out. With the same mix, $\mathrm{rd} = -0.100$.

### A real-life example

Online A/B tests often start with a small share of visitors in the treatment and raise it later. Crook et al.
(2009) report that occurrences of Simpson's paradox in such tests "are not uncommon, and we have seen them
happen multiple times in real life", and illustrate it with conversion rates over two days, the treatment
($Z = 1$) receiving 1% of visitors on Friday ($X = 1$) and 50% on Saturday ($X = 0$), with $Y = 1$ a
conversion:

| $X$          | $Z$ | Visitors | Conversions | $\mathrm{pr}(Y = 1 \mid Z, X)$ |
| :----------- | --: | -------: | ----------: | -----------------------------: |
| 1 (Friday)   |   1 |   10,000 |         230 |                         0.0230 |
| 1 (Friday)   |   0 |  990,000 |      20,000 |                         0.0202 |
| 0 (Saturday) |   1 |  500,000 |       6,000 |                         0.0120 |
| 0 (Saturday) |   0 |  500,000 |       5,000 |                         0.0100 |

conversions up. Conversion rates are near 1%, so every vector is nearly flat and the vertical axis is

</figcaption>

On each day $A_x$ is the steeper vector, $\mathrm{rd}_1 = 0.0028$ and $\mathrm{rd}_0 = 0.0020$: the treatment
converts better. But it received 10,000 of its 510,000 visitors on Friday, so $A_1$ is a stub at the origin
and $A$ is nearly $A_0$, the flatter Saturday direction, while the control received 990,000 of its 1,490,000
on Friday and $B$ leans toward the steeper $B_1$. So

$$
\mathrm{rd} = \frac{6230}{510000} - \frac{25000}{1490000} = -0.0046.
$$

The treatment converts better on each day and worse overall. The reversal holds while
$\mathrm{pr}(X = 1 \mid Z = 1) < 0.434$; a treatment share of traffic that stays the same on both days would
give both arms the same mix. (The paper prints 1.20% for 6,230/510,000, which is 1.22%.)

Crook, T., Frasca, B., Kohavi, R., and Longbotham, R. (2009). Seven pitfalls to avoid when running controlled
experiments on the web. _Proceedings of the 15th ACM SIGKDD International Conference on Knowledge Discovery
and Data Mining_, 1105-1114. [doi:10.1145/1557019.1557139](https://doi.org/10.1145/1557019.1557139)
