# Fisher's test and the p-value

## Example 1.1

[Bertrand and Mullainathan (2004)](#ref-bertrand-2004) sent 4,870 fictitious resumes to help-wanted ads in
Boston and Chicago. Each resume carried a name chosen at random to sound White ($Z = 1$) or Black ($Z = 0$),
and $Y = 1$ if the employer called back. `fisher.test` gives $p = 4.759 \times 10^{-5}$ for their table:

|         | $Y = 1$ | $Y = 0$ |
| :------ | ------: | ------: |
| $Z = 1$ |     235 |   2,200 |
| $Z = 0$ |     157 |   2,278 |

### a. The null hypothesis

Let $X$ be the number of callbacks among the resumes with White-sounding names. Each group has 2,435 resumes
and the two groups have 392 callbacks between them, so $X$ fixes the table:

|         |   $Y = 1$ |             $Y = 0$ |
| :------ | --------: | ------------------: |
| $Z = 1$ |       $X$ |         2,435 - $X$ |
| $Z = 0$ | 392 - $X$ | 2,435 - (392 - $X$) |

The p-value assumes that the name made no difference to any resume. Each resume would then have been called
back, or not, whatever name it carried. The 392 callbacks are fixed, and the random assignment only decides
which 2,435 resumes carry White-sounding names. $X$ is the number of called-back resumes that land in that
pile.

### b. Dealing the names again

I deal the names again at random, keeping the 392 called-back resumes, and record $X$ for each deal (Figure
1). The exact mean and standard deviation of $X$ come from the R package:

```r
> pkgload::load_all("packages/causalding")
> callbacks <- resume_callbacks()
> c(callbacks$null$expected, callbacks$null$sd)
[1] 196.000000   9.493693
```

The deals cluster around 196 with a standard deviation of 9.5, and 99.3% of them fall between 171 and 221. The
center is half the callbacks because the two piles have the same size. The experiment's 235 lies 39 above the
center, or 4.1 standard deviations, and on average 0.48 deals in 10,000 are as extreme. The histogram is
random, so it only approximates the exact distribution of part c.

### c. The exact distribution

Every deal is equally likely, so $\mathrm{pr}(X = x)$ is the fraction of deals that put $x$ of the 392
called-back resumes, and $2435 - x$ of the 4,478 others, into the first pile:

$$
\mathrm{pr}(X = x) = \binom{392}{x} \binom{4478}{2435 - x} \bigg/ \binom{4870}{2435}.
$$

This is the hypergeometric distribution. The two piles have the same size, so
$\mathrm{pr}(X = x) = \mathrm{pr}(X = 392 - x)$ and the distribution is symmetric about 196. The two-sided
p-value adds the probabilities of all counts no more likely than the observed one, which here are the two
tails:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p &= \mathrm{pr}(X \ge 235) + \mathrm{pr}(X \le 157) \\
&= 0.0000238 + 0.0000238 \\
&= 0.0000476.
\end{aligned}
$$

This is the $4.759 \times 10^{-5}$ of `fisher.test`:

```r
> unlist(callbacks$p_value)
       upper        lower    two_sided
2.379374e-05 2.379374e-05 4.758747e-05
> fisher.test(matrix(c(235, 157, 2200, 2278), nrow = 2))$p.value
[1] 4.758747e-05
```

Dealing the names and counting how often the result is as extreme is the Fisher randomization test of
[Chapter 3](../part2/chapter03.mdx); Problem 3.4 shows that for a two-by-two table it coincides with Fisher's
exact test. The book also mentions `chisq.test`, an asymptotic test of the same hypothesis.

### d. Reading the number

If the names made no difference to any resume, a random assignment of the names would produce a gap at least
as large as 235 against 157, in either direction, with probability 0.0000476, or about 5 times in 100,000.
This is not the probability that the names make no difference, since the calculation assumes it. In the words
of the ASA statement, "P-values do not measure the probability that the studied hypothesis is true, or the
probability that the data were produced by random chance alone"
([Wasserstein and Lazar, 2016](#ref-wasserstein-2016)).

Nor is the p-value an effect size. The effect here is the difference in callback rates, 3.2 percentage points,
and the same p-value could come from a smaller effect measured on more resumes. As the
[ASA statement](#ref-wasserstein-2016) puts it, "A p-value, or statistical significance, does not measure the
size of an effect or the importance of a result." The calculation is exact because the names were assigned at
random, so the deals are the outcomes the experiment could have produced under the null hypothesis.

### e. The design

The deals treat the 4,870 resumes as one deck, as `fisher.test` does. The experiment was randomized more
finely. Each ad typically received two higher-quality and two lower-quality resumes, and one of each pair was
drawn at random to receive an African-American name ([Bertrand and Mullainathan, 2004](#ref-bertrand-2004)). A
test that follows this design deals the names within each pair, as in the matched-pairs experiment of
[Chapter 7](../part2/chapter07.mdx). Its p-value could differ from the one above; I have not computed it.
