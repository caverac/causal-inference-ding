# Fisher's test and the p-value

I have been burnt by p-values so many times, that I think it is worth dropping some visuals here, maybe it
will help somebody. Example 1.1 of the book ends with `fisher.test` reporting `p-value = 4.759e-05`. This page
aims to explain what that number means, specifically in the context of this experiment.

## The experiment

[Bertrand and Mullainathan (2004)](#ref-bertrand-2004) sent 4,870 fictitious resumes to job ads in Boston and
Chicago. Each resume carried a name chosen at random to sound White ($Z = 1$) or Black ($Z = 0$), and the
outcome is whether the employer called back ($Y = 1$).

|         | $Y = 1$ | $Y = 0$ |
| :------ | ------: | ------: |
| $Z = 1$ |     235 |   2,200 |
| $Z = 0$ |     157 |   2,278 |

Let's also define $X$ to be the number of callbacks among the resumes with White-sounding names ($Z = 1$). The
table above can be rewritten as

|         |   $Y = 1$ |             $Y = 0$ |
| :------ | --------: | ------------------: |
| $Z = 1$ |       $X$ |         2,435 - $X$ |
| $Z = 0$ | 392 - $X$ | 2,435 - (392 - $X$) |

## The question the p-value answers

Some resumes are more attractive to employers than others, so even if employers ignored names, the two groups
would not get the same number of callbacks: _chance_ decides which resumes carry which names. The p-value asks
whether _chance of this kind_ can account for a gap as large as 235 against 157.

To make the statement "_chance of this kind_" more precise, suppose that the name made no difference to any
resume: each resume would have been called back, or not, whatever name it carried. Then the 392 callbacks did
not depend on the names at all, and the random assignment only decided which pile each resume went into, 2,435
with White-sounding names and 2,435 with Black-sounding names. Under this hypothesis, $X$ is the number of
called-back resumes that happen to land in the first pile when the names are dealt at random.

The deals cluster around 196, half of the 392 callbacks, with a standard deviation of 9.5, and 99.3% of them
land between 171 and 221. The experiment's 235 is 39 above the center, or 4.1 standard deviations. On average,
only 0.48 deals in 10,000 are as extreme as the experiment.

## The exact probability

Counting deals only approximates the probabilities. Every deal is equally likely, so the probability that
$X = x$ is the share of deals that put $x$ of the 392 called-back resumes, and $2435 - x$ of the 4,478 others,
into the first pile:

$$
\mathrm{pr}(X = x) = \binom{392}{x} \binom{4478}{2435 - x} \bigg/ \binom{4870}{2435},
$$

the hypergeometric distribution!

<figcaption>

**Figure 2.** How the p-value is computed. The bars (top) are the exact probabilities $\mathrm{pr}(X = x)$ of
each count if names make no difference; the points (bottom) are the same probabilities on a log scale, where
the tails become visible. The vertical line marks a count $x$, at first the observed 235. The p-value adds the
probabilities of every count at least as unlikely as $x$: those on or below the dashed line, drawn filled. At
235 these are the two tails, $x \ge 235$ and $x \le 157$, which add up to 0.0000476. Move the slider, or drag
across the chart, to see the p-value other outcomes would have had: at $x = 215$, for example, it is 0.0512.

</figcaption>

</Figure>

At the experiment's $x = 235$, the probability of a count at least as large is
$\mathrm{pr}(X \ge 235) = 0.0000238$. Counts as far off in the other direction are just as unlikely: the piles
have the same size, so the distribution is symmetric about 196, and $\mathrm{pr}(X \le 157) = 0.0000238$ too.
The two-sided p-value adds both,

$$
p = \mathrm{pr}(X \ge 235) + \mathrm{pr}(X \le 157) = 0.0000476,
$$

which is the `4.759e-05` printed by `fisher.test`. In general, R adds up the probabilities of all the counts
no more likely than the observed one, which here are the two tails.

Dealing the names at random and asking how often the result is as extreme is the Fisher randomization test of
[Chapter 3](../part2/chapter03.mdx); Problem 3.4 shows that for a two-by-two table it coincides with Fisher's
exact test.

## Reading the number

- **What it says.** If the names made no difference to any resume, a random assignment of the names would
  produce a gap at least as large as the observed one, in either direction, with probability 0.0000476: about
  5 times in 100,000.
- **What it does not say.** It is not the probability that the names make no difference, nor the probability
  that the gap is due to chance, since the calculation assumes that the names make no difference. In the words
  of the ASA statement, "P-values do not measure the probability that the studied hypothesis is true, or the
  probability that the data were produced by random chance alone"
  ([Wasserstein and Lazar, 2016](#ref-wasserstein-2016)).
- **It is not an effect size.** The same p-value could come from a small effect measured on many resumes. The
  size of the effect here is the difference in callback rates, 3.2 percentage points. As the
  [ASA statement](#ref-wasserstein-2016) puts it, "A p-value, or statistical significance, does not measure
  the size of an effect or the importance of a result."
- **Random assignment is what makes it exact.** Because the names were assigned at random, the deals are
  exactly the outcomes the experiment could have produced if names made no difference. Without random
  assignment, the same arithmetic would lose this justification.

## The design, more precisely

The deals above treat the 4,870 resumes as one deck, as `fisher.test` does with the two-by-two table. The
experiment was randomized more finely: each ad typically received two higher-quality and two lower-quality
resumes, and one of each pair was drawn at random to receive an African-American name
([Bertrand and Mullainathan, 2004](#ref-bertrand-2004)). A test that follows this design deals the names
within each pair, as in the matched-pairs experiment of [Chapter 7](../part2/chapter07.mdx).
